## **Summary**

Finding Product-Market Fit (PMF) requires identifying a specific individual who exhibits pull, defined as an urgent operational priority that makes it unnatural for them not to buy a product. Pull is governed by The PULL Framework, which requires four simultaneous conditions: a specific Project on a buyer's active to-do list, an Unavoidable reason requiring immediate execution, a clear List of considered options, and specific Limitations that leave all existing options inadequate. True pull exists independently of founders and cannot be generated through general pain points, value propositions, or feature demonstrations. To locate pull, founders must gain firsthand operational experience in target environments, test simple pull hypotheses directly with prospects, and avoid traps such as formal design partnerships or upfront Software Development. Sales calls must prioritize structured discovery over persuasion, utilizing concise, direct cold outreach. When prospects lack pull for an initial hypothesis, founders should execute diagnostic rejections using open-ended queries or direct field engagement. Once initial buyers are secured, retention depends on establishing a critical usage threshold where churn becomes unlikely, enabling founders to operate the business as a repeatable case study factory across pipeline, sales, and delivery.

## **Core Mechanics of the Pull Framework**

Pull is a property held by an individual person rather than an entire enterprise. For example, enterprise software is sold to a specific stakeholder, such as the head of legal at **SpaceX**, who faces an uncompleted project, rather than to the corporation itself. Buyers with pull act like a coiled spring, demonstrating immediate urgency to acquire any solution that unblocks their project.

"One of the main mistakes people make is thinking that pull is a thing that a company has, it's a thing a person has."

The PULL Framework structures the exact criteria required to establish real buyer pull. All four components must be present simultaneously; if any single element is missing, prospects will either complain without buying or rely on internal workarounds.

| Framework Component | Definition | Practical Operational Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Project | A specific task or job to be done on an individual buyer's active to-do list. | Financial advisors attempting to minimize time spent taking meeting notes and entering CRM data. |
| Unavoidable | A compelling imperative forcing the project to be completed immediately rather than deferred. | Regulatory compliance mandates where failure to record CRM notes risks legal prosecution. |
| List of Options | The full set of existing tools, internal teams, or market alternatives considered for the project. | Generic AI notetaking applications currently available on the market. |
| Limitations | Deficiencies that prevent current options from successfully executing the project. | Lack of CRM integration and lack of compliance with regulatory standards. |

The necessity of all four elements is demonstrated when comparing adjacent markets. While financial advisors buying from **Jump** possessed all four elements, insurance agents had the same project but lacked the unavoidable element because CRM note entry was not legally required. Consequently, insurance agents defaulted to their existing option of omitting notes entirely.

## **Discovery, Sales Execution, and Cold Outreach**

Founders must formulate a clear pull hypothesis and test it directly against market reality before building software. Validating a hypothesis requires evaluating whether target prospects make immediate, pull shaped commitments to purchase.

Step 1: Acquire Firsthand Experience  
Observe or perform target workflows directly to identify precise operational bottlenecks.

Step 2: Formulate the Pull Hypothesis  
Draft a two slide deck defining the target project, urgency, existing limitations, and conceptual solution.

Step 3: Execute Direct Cold Outreach  
Contact 100 to 150 prospects weekly via concise, intriguing messages without complex sales templates.

Step 4: Conduct Structured Discovery Calls  
Run 30 minute calls focused on confirming buyer project context rather than presenting full product demos.

Step 5: Test Transactional Intent  
Require prospects to make a direct buying commitment or explicit agreement to advance implementation.

Cold outreach achieves optimal performance when founders avoid generic sales templates or automated personalization tools. Sending simple messages on **LinkedIn** yields reliable baseline conversion metrics:

* Weekly connection attempts: 100 to 150 target prospects  
* Connection acceptance rate: 30% to 40%  
* Message response rate: 30% to 50%

During the initial 30 minute sales call, founders should maintain a posture of relaxed discovery. The call begins by asking why the prospect agreed to meet, allowing them to outline their pull context directly. Founders then establish an agenda and secure explicit permission to ask discovery questions regarding the buyer's project, timeline urgency, current options, and tool limitations.

## **Diagnostic Rejection Tactics and Operational Pivots**

When discovery calls yield lukewarm feedback, enthusiastic praise without commitment, or requests to defer purchases to future quarters, founders must recognize that pull is absent. In these scenarios, founders should deploy specific diagnostic tactics to locate real pull:

* The Hail Mary Question: When a proposed hypothesis fails, founders immediately ask a diagnostic question: "Is there anything on your to-do list that you would pay me to do right now?" This open-ended prompt allows buyers to reveal actual urgent projects.  
* Goblin Mode Engagement: Founders undertake intensive, direct real-world actions to get directly in front of buyers, such as traveling unannounced to industry conferences or conducting door to door sales visits to target facilities.  
* Avoidance of Design Partnerships: Founders should decline non-paying design partnerships. Design partners frequently offer advice without commercial intent, whereas actual buyers with urgent projects demand immediate execution.  
* Differentiated Positioning over Experience: Building products with improved user interfaces in established markets fails unless poor usability actively blocks the buyer from completing an unavoidable project. For example, software teams switching from **Jira** to **Linear** experience active operational friction from interface speed, whereas corporate buyers evaluating HR tools rarely change vendors based on interface design alone.

## **Delivery, Retention, and the Case Study Factory**

After securing initial sales, delivery must be managed directly by founders. Every buyer is assumed guaranteed to churn until they reach a specific usage milestone, designated as threshold X, after which churning becomes highly unlikely. For example, a restaurant retention software startup determined that delivering a preventable staff resignation alert within the first two weeks served as threshold X, making subsequent churn rare.

When horizontal software products experience severe customer churn, analyzing retention sub-segments often exposes vertical niches exhibiting dense pull. In one instance, a horizontal workflow product suffering 90% customer churn discovered that freight forwarders retained at high rates for a specific process, prompting a complete pivot to serve that single vertical.

Scaling a startup requires building a case study factory designed to produce one identical, successful customer outcome repeatedly. Startups apply the theory of constraints across the three core stages of the factory model:

1. Pipeline Stage: Systematizing cold outreach and referral channels to generate continuous prospect calls.  
2. Sales Stage: Converting prospect calls into paying customers by framing solutions around buyer projects and setting prices based on alternative costs or value generation.  
3. Delivery Stage: Onboarding customers directly to ensure every user reaches threshold X and becomes an active reference.

By evaluating operations through the case study factory model, founders isolate the primary bottleneck constraining company growth at any given moment, directing all organizational resources toward resolving that single operational constraint.
